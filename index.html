I am a Ph.D. candidate in Economics at the University of Colorado Boulder, advised by Tania Barham. I am an applied microeconomist working in health economics, public economics, and the economics of aging and household finance. My research asks how older households manage their wealth, and how health shocks, public policy, and the capacity to manage money shape financial security late in life.

Research

Job Market Paper
Losing the Household CFO: Spousal Exit and Late-Life Wealth
Draft available on request

Within couples, one spouse typically manages the household's wealth and finances. This paper estimates what it costs older households when that spouse, the household's CFO, exits and the survivor is forced to take over. Using the Health and Retirement Study's financial-respondent designation and 2,422 spousal deaths, along with 1,635 long-term nursing-home entries and functional incapacitations (4,057 first spousal exits in total), I estimate a difference-in-differences design that compares households that lose their CFO spouse with households that lose the non-CFO spouse to the same events. A stacked triple-difference design that exploits the timing of exits corroborates the estimates. When the CFO spouse dies, the surviving household's net worth falls by $70,994 on average over the following six years relative to comparison households ($76,715 across all spousal exits), a loss of about 12 percent of baseline net worth (roughly 17 percent in log terms) and more than the average decline comparison households experience after the same event. The financial-wealth loss sits mainly in stocks, bonds, and individual retirement accounts, is largest when the departed CFO was cognitively normal, and is partly recovered only when a cognitively normal survivor takes over. The evidence points to neglect, accounts left untended after an unplanned succession, rather than liquidation or transfers.

Working Papers

When Simpler Taxes Meet Aging Minds: Cognitive Decline and Portfolio Reallocation after a State Income Tax Reform
Third Year Paper Award, Department of Economics, University of Colorado Boulder (2024–2025)
Abstract

Older Americans hold most of the nation's equity wealth, yet many face cognitive decline that may impair their response to changing financial incentives. I study North Carolina's 2014 income tax reform, which replaced a three-bracket progressive schedule with a flat 5.8% rate, lowering and simplifying the tax on investment income. Using a balanced panel from the Health and Retirement Study and a difference-in-differences design with inverse probability weights, I find that the reform raised stock market participation by 3.0 percentage points and equity holdings by roughly 35%, while safe asset and IRA balances fell by about a quarter. A triple-difference design shows that the response is concentrated among cognitively intact respondents. Those whose word recall declined by 20% or more before the reform did not reallocate on any margin. Because the reform simplified rather than complicated the tax schedule, the results indicate that the capacity to recognize and act on a changed environment, not tax complexity, constrains older households' portfolio adjustment.

Publications

Does Lawyers' Ability Enhance Litigation Success? Evidence from Korean Tax Appeal Cases
with Inyeob Ji. Korean Journal of Law and Economics, 18(3): 209–230, 2021 (in Korean)
Abstract

The present article investigates determinants of litigation outcomes. To this end, it examines Korean Supreme Court decisions from which logit regression models are formed and estimated. Its findings suggest that legal representation by large law firms and litigation attorneys (as opposed to advisory attorneys) contribute to litigation success for plaintiff taxpayers. Also, it appears that plaintiff taxpayers have a higher probability of a favorable judgment in national tax cases as opposed to regional tax cases.
